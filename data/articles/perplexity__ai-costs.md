AI costs are what a team pays to use AI tools. They fall into three types:

Take a hypothetical 10-person team paying $25 per person per month for an AI research tool.  The subscription is a fixed $250. In busy months, two analysts run out of included credits and the team buys $100 more, so the bill runs to $350. Rollout adds 18 hours of staff time that never appears on an invoice: eight hours for an operations lead to connect the tool to the company's sign-in system, and an hour of training for each of the 10 team members.

Free plans charge nothing but cap how much each person can use the tool, and they usually leave out the most capable models and some features. When a person reaches the cap, the tool stops handling that type of request or switches to a less capable model until the limit resets.

A seat is a license for one named user, and per-seat plans charge a set price for each seat, billed monthly or annually. Team versions usually add admin controls, such as deciding who has access. The bill scales with headcount rather than with use, so a person who runs two queries a week costs the same as one who runs 200.

Premium tiers are higher-priced individual plans with higher usage limits, more capable models, or more advanced features. They're still priced per person, so a team pays the higher rate only for each person it upgrades.

Credit plans include a monthly allowance that tasks use up at different rates. Usually complex tasks use more credits than simple ones. When the allowance runs out, people can wait for it to reset or buy more. Unused credits often expire. Credits usually sit on top of a subscription rather than replacing it.

Teams that build AI into their own software or workflows pay through an API, which lets software send requests to an AI model directly.

API usage is billed per token (small chunk of text). A short, common word is usually one token, while longer words are split into two or more.

Both the text sent to the model and the text it generates count toward the bill. Some APIs add per-request fees on top.

Enterprise plans combine seats with advanced security and administration features, such as single sign-on and controls over how long data is stored. Pricing is typically annual, and listed per seat or set by quote. Some enterprise AI plans are add-ons that require a separate base license, so their cost comes on top of software the company already pays for.

Outcome-based pricing charges for a completed result rather than for access or usage, so the bill depends on how the vendor defines a result. Intercom’s Fin, for example, bills only once per customer conversation.

Standard individual plans for the most widely used AI tools cost about $20 a month. Premium individual tiers run $100 to $500. Business seats cost about $14 to $40 per user per month.

Four factors shape what a team spends on AI tools:

On seat-based plans, the bill depends on how many people need their own login, not on how many people benefit from the tool's output. A research lead might use an AI tool to produce a report that 10 colleagues read, but only the research lead will need a seat.

Team plans often set a minimum number of seats, so a very small team can end up paying for more seats than it uses.

Every step an AI tool takes requires computing work, and tasks with more steps cost more to run.

A quick question takes one step. The model reads the query and writes an answer. A research task takes many. The tool runs several searches, reads the sources it finds, and writes a longer report. An agent task can take the most, since the agent plans its approach, uses other tools, checks its results, and repeats steps until the task is done.

More capable models cost more to run because they do more work for every answer. They're usually larger, so producing each word takes more computing power, and many write out their reasoning privately before answering, which adds work the user never sees.

Plans pass that cost on in two ways. Some reserve their most capable models for higher-priced tiers. Others include them but count their use more heavily against usage limits, so a person who uses the most capable model reaches the plan's limit sooner than one using a lighter model.

Anthropic’s Claude, for example, does both: its Opus models aren't available on the free plan, and the model a person chooses affects how much they can do before reaching a limit.

Security requirements can set the minimum plan a company needs, regardless of how much the team uses AI.

Features such as single sign-on, audit logs (records of who did what in the tool), custom data retention, and commitments not to train models on company content are usually reserved for business and enterprise tiers.

Claude's Pro and Max plans, for example, include none of the security and administration features on its pricing page, while its Team plan adds single sign-on and doesn't train models on a team's content by default. A company that requires these controls has to buy a business plan even for light users.

Setting up an AI tool takes administrator time before anyone can use it. Many tools connect to apps the company already uses, such as email or file storage, so an administrator has to decide which connections to allow and who can use them. This work continues after launch, too, as administrators need to add and remove people and review settings when the vendor adds new features.

Companies need to check whether their data will be used to train models, where it's stored, and which security standards the vendor meets. Teams often buy AI tools on their own. For example, marketing and sales might each pay for a different tool that does the same kind of research or drafting. In addition, many products a company already pays for, such as email, document, and meeting software, now include AI features, so a new standalone tool could duplicate capabilities the company already has. In both cases, the company could end up paying twice for the same features.

Instead of using the vendor’s monthly pricing to estimate AI costs, teams can use cost per task as a more accurate estimate. Cost per task refers to the cost of the plan, including any extra usage, divided by the number of tasks the team will run on the AI tool.

To estimate the AI cost per task and compare plans before you buy:

Choose three to five recurring tasks that take up the most team time and that the team wants AI to help with, such as preparing competitor briefs or summarizing customer calls. For each one, note whether it's a quick question with a short answer or a bigger job that means searching, reading several sources, or working through several steps, since plans usually limit bigger jobs more tightly.

Use records the team already has, such as delivered reports, closed tickets, or meeting calendars, to estimate a monthly number for each group.

Using a free plan or trial, have two or three people run their tasks for a week or two. Note which tasks the tool handles well, how long people spend reviewing and correcting its output, and whether anyone reaches a usage limit.

On each vendor's pricing page and help center, find the limit for each task type, whether limits apply per person or are shared, and what happens at a limit. If these details aren't published, ask the vendor's sales team.

Multiply the base price by the number of seats, then add the cost of any tasks beyond the plan's limits. Divide by the monthly task count from step 2, counting only the tasks the trial showed the tool handles well.

Plans with similar prices can produce very different results here, depending on how much work each includes and how many of the team's tasks each tool handles well.

These savings come at three points: before buying a tool, at each renewal, and in day-to-day use.

Before buying a new AI tool, ask IT or procurement which AI tools the company already pays for and which AI features come with software it already uses, such as its email and document suite.

Then list the AI subscriptions team members pay for individually and expense, noting the tasks each one handles and when it renews. Where two tools cover the same tasks, use the cost-per-task comparison above to choose one, and switch at the other tool's renewal date to avoid paying for both.

Ask vendors about discounts for eligible organizations, since several discount their business and enterprise plans. Expect the largest discounts to require a conversation with the vendor's sales team.

At higher seat counts, ask for a quote rather than paying list price, since enterprise pricing is often negotiable. Beyond the price per seat, ask for these terms:

Before each renewal, review how much each person used the tool over the past few months, using the plan's usage reports if available. Remove seats for people who rarely use the tool, and move people who never reach standard limits from premium tiers to standard ones. Do this before the renewal date, since annual contracts often lock in the seat count for another year.

Switch to annual billing once a trial and a few months of use show steady demand. Many AI plans cost less per month when billed annually, in exchange for paying for a full year upfront.

On plans that offer a choice of model, train users to choose a faster, lighter model for quick tasks and save the most capable models for complex work.

A quick task has a short answer or makes a small change, such as rewriting an email or answering a factual question. A complex task means reasoning through several steps, such as analyzing multiple documents. When unsure, start with the lighter model and switch only if its answer doesn’t meet requirements. Usage limits then last longer, which can keep heavy users from needing a premium tier or extra usage charges.

Free AI plans are fine for testing a tool, but most aren't designed for company data. On many free and individual plans, conversations can be used to train the vendor's AI models unless the user opts out. Business plans usually exclude company data from training by default.

Sharing one individual AI subscription across a team is usually a poor fit. Everyone shares the usage limits and conversation history. Many vendors' terms also restrict individual accounts to one person, so a team plan is usually the safer choice even for two or three people.

For everyday knowledge work, buying an off-the-shelf AI tool is almost always cheaper than building custom AI. Building means paying for development, the computing power to run AI models, and ongoing maintenance on top of usage costs. It makes sense mainly when existing tools can't handle a task or a company needs AI inside its own product.

AI costs are everything a team pays to use AI tools, including subscriptions, usage charges, and rollout time. Token spend is the metered cost of using AI models through an API, billed per token (a small chunk of text).
