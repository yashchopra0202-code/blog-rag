Here are some more examples of how to evaluate AI output.

“The U.S. Small Business Administration offers grants of up to $50,000 for new retail businesses.”

Confirm the program actually exists, eligibility requirements, current amount, application deadline, and whether it is a grant rather than a loan or local program.

“A 2025 Gallup survey found that 62% of customers prefer AI-powered support.”

Open the cited source. Check that it’s real, recent, correctly attributed, and actually supports the exact statistic and wording.

“Your company must retain customer chat records for seven years.”

Verify the jurisdiction, industry, record type, and current legal rule with authoritative guidance or qualified counsel. AI may flatten state, federal, international, or sector-specific requirements into one false universal rule.

“You can deduct the full cost of this equipment in the year you buy it.”

Confirm the relevant tax year, business entity, purchase type, limits, and accounting treatment with IRS guidance and a tax professional.

“If conversion rises by 15%, annual revenue will increase by $180,000.”

Recalculate the math and scrutinize the assumptions: current traffic, baseline conversion rate, average order value, churn, sales capacity, seasonality, and whether “15%” means percentage points or relative growth.

“The contract permits termination with 30 days’ notice.”

Read the original clause and linked provisions. AI can omit exceptions, notice requirements, renewal provisions, remedies, or definitions that change the practical meaning.

“You should automate refund approvals to save staff time.”

Decide whether the workflow is reversible, whether errors could hurt customers or finances, what approval thresholds are appropriate, and who owns exceptions. A sensible-sounding recommendation still requires business judgment.

AI literacy is the ability to understand, use, evaluate, and make informed decisions about AI systems and their output. That goes beyond just writing successful prompts. AI-literate people can recognize where AI is useful, protect sensitive information, check important claims, understand the limits of an answer, and know when a person must make the final decision.

An example of AI literacy is using an AI tool to draft a competitor analysis, then opening every cited source to verify prices, product features, dates, and quotations before using the analysis in a sales presentation. The most important part of literacy is the ability to spot what needs checking, validate it against reliable evidence, and revise or discard unsupported claims.

Improve AI literacy by practicing with real, low-risk work. Tutorials are helpful, but they are no substitute for hands-on practice. Choose an AI tool and a repeatable task, such as summarizing a document, researching a competitor, or organizing notes. Then review the output carefully by tracing where information came from, verifying important facts and citations, and correct any errors. Over time, build the habit of deciding which tasks AI can assist with, which need human review, and which should be reserved with humans. The goal is to use AI confidently, safely, and with sound judgment.

Start with the claims that matter most. Check whether the source exists and supports the exact statement. Confirm that names, dates, products, and figures are correct and presented accurately. Make sure the information is current. Involve a qualified reviewer for legal, medical, financial, security, employment, or other high-stakes decisions. AI-generated citations can make the process faster, but a citation should still be opened and checked.
